rocm-racer

Training a PPO agent to drive Need for Speed: Underground 2 on my RX 7900 XTX

A local ROCm training dojo for a PS2 racing game

Paused / Work in Progress

rocm-racer is my open-source reinforcement-learning project to train an autonomous driving agent against Need for Speed: Underground 2 running in the PCSX2 emulator, entirely on my own hardware — an AMD Radeon RX 7900 XTX on Arch Linux, driven by AMD ROCm 7.1. No cloud GPUs, no rented compute. Just a PS2 emulator, a PPO agent, and a graphics card that was originally bought for gaming.

Before this turns into a highlight reel, the honest version: this project is paused. Other things are taking priority right now, and there's an unresolved telemetry problem — reading reliable vehicle coordinates out of the PS2's memory — that I never got to the bottom of. What follows is a snapshot of the architecture, the reverse-engineering rabbit hole I fell into, and exactly where it's stuck.

Why a 2004 racing game?

Underground 2 runs on EA Black Box's engine, itself built on Criterion's RenderWare middleware — a platform well documented by two decades of PC cheat-engine and modding communities. It has a free-roam city with a long, simple highway loop that's ideal as a training track, and a 100%-completion save that skips every tutorial and unlock gate. Need for Speed: Most Wanted is planned as a second target since it shares the same engine, but Underground 2 came first.

Reading the PS2's mind: telemetry over PINE

Older emulator memory-hacking tools got away with hardcoding a host address for the emulated console's RAM. PCSX2 dropped that entirely for 64-bit builds with ASLR, so guest memory is now allocated wherever the host OS heap feels like putting it. The fix is PINE (Protocol for Instrumentation of Emulators) — an IPC server built into PCSX2 that speaks in guest physical addresses over a Unix socket and handles the host-translation math internally.

My Python side connects to $XDG_RUNTIME_DIR/pcsx2.sock and reads the PS2's full 32 MB Emotion Engine RAM directly — no ptrace, no /proc/pid/mem, no root. For a full heuristic scan I pull the entire 32 MB block in one shot and reinterpret it as a numpy float32 array, which turns a scan that would take minutes over individual socket reads into something that runs in milliseconds.

When PC cheat-engine offsets lie to you

Because Underground 2 shares its engine across PC, PS2, and GameCube, and because RenderWare's vector-processing requirements force every physics struct onto strict 16-byte boundaries, my working assumption was simple: the PC struct offsets documented by cheat-engine communities for years — position at 0x20, velocity at 0x70, speed at 0x90 — should map straight onto the PS2 build.

They didn't. Every one of the 29–40 candidate structs I scanned read exactly 0.000 at those offsets while the car was doing 90 km/h. The PS2 EA Black Box build uses a different struct layout, a different allocation container, or a console-specific entity header — and there's no public static pointer table for SLUS-21065 to fall back on.

So --calibrate exists instead: an empirical differential scan. Snapshot the full RAM while parked, snapshot again after 3 seconds of throttle, snapshot again after a turn, then intersect every candidate address against physics constraints — a normalized quaternion here, a speed value between 0 and 30 m/s there, position that moves smoothly instead of teleporting. A real run looks like this:

Since the vehicle struct is heap-allocated fresh every session, there's no fixed address to hardcode — the calibrator has to re-anchor itself (or fall back to a cached, direct address) each time. Across enough sessions, scalar speed and the X/Y/Z world position turned out to be solid: position moves ~14 m per half-second at 100 km/h, exactly what it should, and the speed value matches the in-game speedometer. Velocity and rotation are a different story — more on that below.

A virtual gamepad with no GUI clicking

The agent needs to press real buttons. I create a virtual gamepad through /dev/uinput and write PCSX2's SDL3 game_controller_db.txt mapping before the emulator process even starts, so the device is enumerated as a proper controller from the very first SDL init — no manual "map your controller" step in the PCSX2 UI, ever. Every episode reset relaunches this cleanly and deterministically.

Steering and throttle/brake come out of PPO as a continuous 2-vector and get pushed straight onto the virtual analog stick axes at a 10 Hz control loop.

Multimodal PPO: eyes and telemetry

The policy fuses two branches through a custom MultimodalExtractor feeding Stable-Baselines3's MultiInputPolicy:

  • Vision: the PCSX2 window is captured on Wayland via grim + hyprctl (no portal consent dialogs, works unattended across resets), cropped to drop the HUD, reduced to 96×96 grayscale, and stacked 4 frames deep. A small Atari-style CNN (three conv layers, 32→64→64 channels) processes the stack.
  • Telemetry: the 5-value vector — speed and X/Y/Z position — goes through a two-layer MLP.

Both branches are concatenated and projected into a 512-dim feature vector for PPO's actor-critic heads. Training runs with learning_rate=3e-4, n_steps=2048, batch_size=64, on the ROCm device, with optional multi-instance parallel PCSX2 windows tiled across the desktop for faster rollout collection.

Reward shaping

Every step rewards forward speed (capped at 150 km/h, so there's no incentive to floor it past that), applies a small time penalty to create urgency, and gently penalizes jittery steering. Distance from the episode's start position pays out one-time milestone bonuses at 100 m, 250 m and 500 m, plus a larger bonus for clearing the episode's success distance. Episodes terminate early if the car sits under 3 km/h for 2 seconds (stuck) or under 15 km/h for 5 seconds (crawling) — both after a short grace period so the agent has time to get moving from a standing start.

Per the project's own notes, the design also calls for freezing every environment's inputs and saving state around each PPO gradient update — so the car doesn't keep drifting or coasting into a wall while the GPU is busy computing on ROCm — plus an auto-escalating distance goal as the agent gets better. I'm flagging those as documented intent rather than something I re-verified end-to-end line by line while writing this post.

Where it stands

With telemetry-only observations, roughly a quarter of training runs reach the 1000 m distance goal on the highway loop. That's a real, if modest, signal that the reward shaping and control pipeline work. But the project stalled before I could push much further, and the blocker is exactly the "RAM coordinates" problem from the calibration section above:

Telemetry signal Status Notes
Scalar speed Verified Matches the in-game speedometer
Position (X/Y/Z) Verified Smooth across the highway loop, ~1 unit ≈ 1 m
Velocity vector Unreliable Explodes to ±30,000 after a collision — likely a physics scratch buffer, not true velocity
Rotation quaternion Unreliable Degenerate 2D wheel-spin angle, not a real 3D orientation; flips sign every frame in a straight line
Track-progress flag Placeholder Offset not found yet — reward currently uses straight-line distance from the start, not path-relative progress

Without a trustworthy velocity and orientation, there's no clean way to detect a wall collision directly, reward staying pointed the right way down the road, or measure progress along a curved section of track instead of straight-line distance. That's the open problem I never fixed before other priorities took over.

rocm-racer is fully open source at github.com/Erik-Schuetze/rocm-racer. It's paused, not abandoned — the PINE telemetry pipeline, the scripted virtual gamepad, and the multimodal PPO loop are all real and working on real hardware, and the memory-map notes are there for whenever I (or anyone else poking around AMD + RL + emulation) picks the coordinate problem back up.
